1. Course Overview

This application-centered course develops computer vision systems from basic image operations to modern predictive visual intelligence. Students begin with image representation and OpenCV, then implement convolutional neural networks, train YOLO object detectors, apply ResNet transfer learning, fine-tune vision transformers, evaluate vision-language models, and construct a small predictive world-model pipeline. The course emphasizes data curation, reproducibility, robustness, model efficiency, ethical use, and the ability to explain what a system does, why its evaluation is valid, and where uncertainty remains.

Prerequisites. Python programming; basic linear algebra and probability; prior exposure to machine learning is helpful but not required. A short Python/PyTorch readiness module is provided during Week 1.

Instructional pattern. Each class follows a concept → guided demonstration → team laboratory activity → debrief/check → assignment sequence. Most class meetings prioritize implementation, analysis, and troubleshooting rather than extended lecture.

Computing environment. Python, Jupyter/Google Colab, OpenCV, PyTorch, torchvision, a YOLO implementation selected by the instructor, scikit-learn, matplotlib, Hugging Face tools, and Git/GitHub or an equivalent version-control platform. GPU access is recommended but not required for every assignment.

3. Learning Resources

No single textbook is required. Selected chapters, tutorials, documentation, and research papers will be posted in the LMS. Recommended references include:

  • Instructor-provided model cards, dataset cards, reproducibility checklists, and evaluation templates.

Assignment Submission Standard

  • Each assignment must be submitted as a standalone GitHub repository with an accessible narrated demo video; the final project uses a separate repository and final video.
  • The repository must identify the exact release tag and commit hash being graded and must run from a clean environment using the submitted instructions.
  • The repository must include a README, environment or requirements file, source code, configurations, data documentation, results, a reproducibility check, an AI-use disclosure, and DEMO_VIDEO.md.
  • Training, validation, and test data must remain separated; any exception must be disclosed and justified.
  • Results must include class-level or case-level errors, at least one realistic failure condition, and a concise limitation analysis.
  • Images, datasets, code, pretrained weights, and external text must be credited according to their licenses and course rules; credentials, API keys, restricted data, and unauthorized large files may not be committed.

Minimum GitHub Repository Standard

Each assignment must use a separate repository. A repository may be public or private according to instructor direction; private repositories must grant instructor access before the deadline. The minimum structure is:

  • README.md: problem, objectives, installation, data access, execution commands, results, failure analysis, limitations, and authorship.
  • requirements.txt or environment.yml, plus fixed random seeds and hardware or runtime information needed to reproduce results.
  • src/, notebooks/, configs/, tests/, data/README.md, results/, assets/, and docs/ organized so code, data instructions, and evidence are separated.
  • DATASET_CARD.md for every assignment using data; MODEL_CARD.md for A3-A10 and the final project.
  • AI_USE.md describing tools used, prompts or functions delegated, student verification, and any generated material retained in the submission.
  • DEMO_VIDEO.md containing the video URL, exact release tag, commit hash, duration, and a timestamped outline.

Demo Video Standard

  • Begin by showing the assignment title, student name, repository URL, release tag, and commit hash. The video must use the exact version submitted for grading.
  • Briefly state the problem, dataset, model or algorithm, and evaluation criteria; avoid spending most of the recording on slides.
  • Run the principal command or notebook section and demonstrate the system on at least one success case and one meaningful failure or limitation case.
  • Show quantitative results and explain what they mean. A metric screenshot without interpretation is insufficient.
  • Conclude with setup and reproduction instructions, known limitations, responsible-use concerns, and the student contribution when work is completed in a team.
  • Use readable screen resolution, clear narration, and captions. The video may be unlisted but must remain accessible through the end of the grading and appeal period.
  • Minor editing is allowed, but the recording may not conceal failed runs, substitute a different code version, or present another person's execution as the student's own.

8. Final Project

Students complete an individual or small-team project that applies the full vision-system lifecycle: define a problem, curate data, establish a baseline, select an advanced model, evaluate the system, analyze failures, and communicate a defensible conclusion. Teams may contain two or three students, but every student completes an individual code walk-through and oral defense.

Required Technical Scope

  • A clearly stated problem, user need, and success criterion.
  • A documented dataset or data-collection process, including train/validation/test separation and a dataset card.
  • At least one baseline drawn from OpenCV, a small CNN, YOLO, or ResNet.
  • At least one advanced component drawn from vision transformers, VLMs, or predictive/world-model methods.
  • Evaluation using task-appropriate metrics plus robustness, efficiency, and qualitative failure analysis.
  • A discussion of bias, privacy, safety, misuse, licensing, uncertainty, and when human review is required.
  • A standalone GitHub repository with a tagged release, environment file, trained-model instructions, recorded demo video, and reproducible execution path.

Suggested Project Directions

  • Small-object detection in aerial, satellite, traffic, or campus imagery.
  • Visual inspection for manufacturing, infrastructure, or cyber-physical systems.

9. Midterm Practical Assessment

The midterm is an individual, time-limited practical assessment covering image representation, OpenCV, data preparation, CNN concepts, code tracing, metric interpretation, and debugging. Students may be asked to modify a small pipeline, identify leakage or evaluation errors, interpret results, and explain their reasoning. Generative AI tools are not permitted during this assessment unless explicitly authorized.

10. Course Policies

AI-Assisted Work

Students may use generative AI and code-assistance tools for most learning activities and assignments, provided the use is disclosed and the student remains responsible for every submitted claim, line of code, result, and citation. Submitting unverified AI output, fabricated experiments, invented references, or code the student cannot explain is not acceptable.

Short oral checks, live code modifications, and individual assessments will be used to verify mastery. Restrictions for quizzes, the midterm, or other individual assessments will be stated in advance.

Academic Integrity and Reproducibility

Students may discuss concepts and debugging strategies, but submitted work must clearly identify individual and team contributions. Reused code, datasets, pretrained models, figures, and text must be credited. Results must be reproducible from the submitted materials. Fabricated data, altered logs, hidden test-set tuning, and unreported copying are violations of academic integrity.

Late Work

Assignments submitted within 24 hours after the deadline may receive up to a 10% deduction; work submitted between 24 and 72 hours late may receive up to a 25% deduction. Work more than 72 hours late may be accepted only when required for course continuity or supported by an approved accommodation or documented emergency. Project milestones should be submitted on time because they support feedback and team coordination.

Attendance and Laboratory Participation

Because the course is laboratory-centered, students are expected to attend synchronous sessions, participate in demonstrations and peer reviews, and maintain regular progress. Hybrid participation must follow the mode announced for that week. Missing a class does not remove responsibility for code, data, or milestone requirements posted in the LMS.

Data, Safety, and Responsible Use

Students must use datasets and systems consistent with university policy, applicable licenses, privacy requirements, and instructor approval. Projects involving identifiable people, sensitive records, surveillance, high-risk decision-making, or unsafe physical deployment require explicit approval and appropriate safeguards. Course work must not be used to make consequential decisions about real individuals.

Accessibility and Student Support

Students needing academic accommodations should use the university’s established process and communicate approved needs as early as possible. Course materials will be provided in accessible formats when feasible. Students experiencing technical, academic, or personal barriers should contact the instructor and relevant university support services promptly.
